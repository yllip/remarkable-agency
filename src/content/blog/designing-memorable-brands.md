Most brand work is judged in a room where nothing else is competing for attention. The logo sits in the middle of a slide, the colours are chosen, everyone nods. Then the identity goes out into the world and meets an inbox, a shelf, a search results page — and it has to earn a second of attention against everything else on the screen.

## Start with behaviour, not marks

Before we draw anything, we write down how the brand should behave: what it says first, what it refuses to say, how it sounds when something goes wrong. Those decisions constrain the visual work in a useful way. A brand that has committed to being direct doesn't end up with a decorative typeface it can't set body copy in.

## Design the repeated things

People don't remember a brand from the launch campaign. They remember it from the things they see fifty times: the button, the confirmation email, the packaging seal, the way a photo is cropped. We spend most of the design budget on those, and comparatively little on the hero moments.

## Leave room to grow

An identity that only works at one size, in one language, on one background is a liability. We test every mark against the awkward cases early — a favicon, a sponsor wall, a single colour print run — because those are the cases that decide whether a system survives its second year.

The result is rarely the most surprising thing in the category. It's the thing people can still recognise from across the room.
